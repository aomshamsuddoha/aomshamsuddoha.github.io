About

Profile

My research goal is to make computers faster and far more energy efficient. Modern workloads, machine learning above all, spend much of their time and energy moving data between memory and processor. I am interested in hardware that removes this bottleneck through low-power circuit design, in-memory computing and architectures beyond the von Neumann model.

I also look to nature for direction. The human brain learns, recognises and reasons on roughly 20 watts, far less than the hardware we use for artificial intelligence today. Understanding how the brain computes, and mimicking it in silicon through neuromorphic design, is one of the most promising routes to efficient machines.

I am a Lecturer in Electrical and Electronic Engineering at the University of Liberal Arts Bangladesh (ULAB) in Dhaka, where I have also served as Program Coordinator of the B.Sc. in EEE program since February 2025. Over nearly six years I have taught VLSI, solid-state devices, electronic circuits, power electronics and embedded systems at four universities. My research began with my undergraduate thesis on low-power SRAM design using FinFETs, which became my first-author paper. Since then I have co-authored eight peer-reviewed publications, supervised several undergraduate capstone projects, including one on continual learning in spiking neural networks for neuromorphic vision, and served as a technical reviewer for international conferences.

I now want to commit to research full time and begin a PhD for deeper, more focused research experience. I am looking for a PhD position in Electrical and Computer Engineering where I can work on low-power VLSI, in-memory computing and beyond von Neumann computing architectures, along with the solid-state device physics and nanotechnology that underpin them. I am also interested, to a lesser extent, in biomedical and wearable electronics.

Research

Research Interests

One question connects all of my interests: how can we build computers that are faster and use far less energy? I approach it across the whole computing stack, from the physics of a single device up to the architecture of the complete system.

  1. L4Architecture

    Beyond von Neumann and neuromorphic computing

    Architectures that bring memory and processing together to overcome the memory wall, including brain-inspired systems that compute with spiking neurons for fast, energy-efficient learning and inference.

    • Memory wall
    • Neuromorphic
    • Spiking neural networks
  2. L3Memory

    In-memory computing

    Performing computation inside or beside memory arrays such as SRAM, cutting the energy and delay of moving data. This matters most for data-heavy workloads such as machine learning.

    • Compute-in-memory
    • SRAM
    • ML accelerators
  3. L2Circuits

    Low-power VLSI

    Circuit techniques that cut dynamic and leakage power in digital logic and memory, including adiabatic (energy-recovery) logic and FinFET-based design.

    • Adiabatic logic
    • FinFET SRAM
    • Cadence Virtuoso
    Where my research began: B.Sc. thesis and first-author paper
  4. L1Devices and physics

    Solid-state devices and nanotechnology

    The solid-state physics of nanoscale and emerging transistors, such as FinFET and gate-all-around devices, and new materials and device concepts for future computing. Device behaviour sets the limits for every layer above it.

    • Solid-state physics
    • Nanoscale devices
    • Emerging transistors
Where this work applies

  • Machine-learning hardware

    Faster, more energy-efficient chips for training and inference.

  • Biomedical electronics

    Low-power hardware for health monitoring, building on my co-authored work on EEG and MRI analysis.

  • Wearable electronics

    Body-worn devices, where energy efficiency directly sets battery life.

Featured publication

My first paper as first author · Based on my B.Sc. thesis

Design and Performance Analysis of SRAM Circuit Using Adiabatic Logic with FinFET

A. O. M. Shamsuddoha, M. K. Islam and S. N. Biswas

2021 International Conference on Electronics, Communications and Information Technology (ICECIT), Bangladesh, pp. 1–4

This paper applies adiabatic (energy-recovery) logic to an SRAM cell built with FinFET devices and analyses the resulting power and performance. It grew out of my undergraduate thesis, supervised by Prof. Satyendra Nath Biswas. It is special to me as my first paper as first author, and it remains the work most closely aligned with the research I want to pursue in my PhD.

  • SRAM
  • FinFET
  • Adiabatic logic
  • Low-power design
  • Cadence Virtuoso
